## I. Book Argument (The Spine)

### Working title
**RU:** «Читатель как адресат: комментаторские стратегии в русских академических переводах классических текстов Южной Азии»  
**EN:** *The Reader as Addressee: Commentary Strategies in Russian Academic Translations of South Asian Classical Texts*

### Core thesis
Commentary strategy is a translator's implicit theory of the reader — not a response to the source text, but a construction of the target reader's competence, needs, and interpretive horizon.

**Evidence base:** The same 700-verse Sanskrit text (Bhagavadgītā) produces six radically incommensurable commentary apparatuses across 221 years (Petrov 1788 → Blinderman 2020s). The text is a constant; the strategies diverge maximally. The only variable is the reader each translator imagines.

**The indigenous comparator:** The Sanskrit tika tradition named this logic explicitly — the concept of *adhikārin* (the qualified reader, the one entitled to receive a teaching). Every classical Sanskrit commentator from Śaṅkara to Nīlakaṇṭha first determines who is entitled to understand before deciding how to explain. Modern Russian academic translation replicated this logic unconsciously, in three stable forms.

### Three reader-contracts identified in the corpus

| Contract | Reader imagined | Strategy | Russian exemplars | Kazansky type |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Philological** | Specialist who needs the primary evidence laid out | High IAST density, textual variants, grammatical glosses | Kalyanov, Elizarenkova, Burba | А (philological) |
| **Humanist** | Educated non-specialist who needs cultural orientation | Realia + myth + historical context, long explanatory notes | Vasilkov/Neveleva, Grinnser, Averintsev | Б+Г (realia + cultural) |
| **Philosophical** | Reader engaging with the text as a live philosophical problem | Doctrine, system, comparative philosophy, reader as interlocutor | Sementsov, Syrkin, Losev | Г (cultural/philosophical) |

**The fourth contract (indigenous tika):** The *adhikārin* contract — the commentator assumes a student already inside the tradition, reads the Sanskrit word by word, and provides grammatical resolution + theological authorization. This contract is structurally absent from Russian academic translation, which explains why Nīlakaṇṭha's commentary and Kalyanov's footnotes on the same Mahābhārata passage are incommensurable: they are written for readers who do not overlap.

### The meta-argument
Russian Indological commentary is not a provincial or derivative tradition. It developed the same typological range as Russian classical philology (Gasparov/Averintsev/Losev) and Western Indological scholarship (Goldman/Buitenen/Edgerton), but through an independent path. The three reader-contracts are a convergent evolution, not an imitation. This positions the book as a contribution to general translation studies, not only Indology.

- The incommensurability finding: different objects (the word vs. the passage), different authorities (Pāṇini vs. BORI critical edition), different readers

### Chapter 4: Sanskrit and Pali — Two Traditions, One Framework (≈50 pp.)
- Toporov's Dhammapada (1960) as control case: Buddhist Pali vs. Sanskrit epic
- The "false friends" problem across traditions: *dharma* in Pali is not *dharma* in Sanskrit — and Russian translators handled the divergence differently

### Chapter 5: Sanskrit Translators and Their Classical Analogues (≈40 pp.)

- Mapping Sanskrit translators onto the poles: Kalyanov ≈ Gasparov, Vasilkov ≈ Averintsev, Sementsov ≈ Losev
- The convergence argument: Russian Indology reproduced the general typology of Russian academic translation — it is isomorphic, not isolated

### Conclusion: Toward a General Typology (≈30 pp.)

- Digital corpus as the method's future: samskrtam.ru + LLM pipeline

## III. Article Series — Derived from Book Chapters

### Article 1 → ВЯ (Year 1, Russian, highest priority)
**Title:** «Концептуальная непереводимость как переводческая стратегия: санскритские ключевые термины в русских академических переводах»

**Why ВЯ:** This is a linguistics argument with corpus data — quantifiable, reproducible, directly relevant to translation linguistics. It does not require the reader to know Sanskrit.

**Core argument:** Sanskrit has a class of terms (*dharma*, *ātman*, *brahman*, *māyā*, *nirvāṇa*, *yoga*, *bhakti* — ≈25 terms) where the translation problem is not lexical equivalence but ontological incommensurability. Russian academic translators developed three measurable strategies: (1) transliteration + gloss (high IAST density in notes), (2) contextual calque (term translated differently each occurrence, explained in note), (3) domestication (single Russian equivalent, no note). The choice of strategy is a fingerprint of reader-contract: strategy 1 → philological contract; strategy 2 → humanist contract; strategy 3 → philosophical contract.

**Data:** Extract all notes referencing the 25-term lexicon from the full 17,863-note corpus. Compute: frequency per translator, strategy distribution, note length by term. This is an automated extraction task.

**Distinguishes itself from existing work:** No prior study has approached this quantitatively in Russian Indology. The closest is Kazansky's "false friends" discussion — but his is about formal equivalence (European languages). The Sanskrit case is qualitatively different: the incommensurability is conceptual, not lexical.

**Structure:** ≈25 pp.
1. The problem: why Sanskrit terms resist equivalence (not a failure but a feature of the tradition)
2. The 25-term lexicon — selection criteria
3. Corpus method: automated extraction, three-strategy classification
4. Results: translator profiles on the untranslatability axis
5. The reader-contract interpretation
6. Conclusion: implications for translation theory

**Эрман already in corpus (758 notes).**

### Automated extraction pipeline

1. **Input:** Raw text of translation + notes (OCR'd or digital)
2. **Step 1 — Boundary detection:** LLM identifies note anchor + note text pairs
3. **Step 2 — Classification:** LLM classifies each note by 4 types (А/Б/В/Г) + 5 axes (I–V) using the 8-category schema from PROMPT_TEMPLATE.md
4. **Step 3 — "False friends" extraction:** Regex + LLM flags any note containing the 25-term lexicon
5. **Step 4 — IAST detection:** Regex detects presence of diacritical characters
6. **Step 5 — Length + anchor-unit:** Automated measurement
7. **Output:** JSON per translator, structured as the existing corpus

**Validation:** Sample 50 notes per translator for human review — not annotation, just checking LLM classification accuracy. One session per translator.

### "False friends" lexicon — first pass (25 terms)

*dharma, ātman, brahman (neut.), Brahman (masc.), māyā, karma, nirvāṇa, mokṣa, saṃsāra, yoga, bhakti, śūnya, ahiṃsā, tapas, śakti, guṇa (triguṇa), varṇa, āśrama, yajña, mantra, ṛta, satya, prajñā, buddhi, ahaṃkāra*

Extraction query: for each translator, how many notes reference each term, what strategy is used (transliterate / calque / domesticate / omit), what is the average note length for that term.
